YOUR INTERNAL CLOCK

A simplified demo of Gibbon's (1977) Scalar Expectancy Theory, the "pacemaker–accumulator" model of interval timing discussed in Topic 9.

The idea: a steady internal pacemaker fires pulses. Your attention acts as a switch that lets those pulses reach an accumulator, a running tally. When the tally reaches a remembered target, you respond, that's your brain's best guess that "the interval" has elapsed. Try it below. Then flip the attention mode and watch what "time flies when you're having fun" actually looks like from the inside.

Try 30 trials at T = 2s, note the CV. Then try 30 trials at T = 8s. The spread (SD) will be much bigger in seconds, but the CV should land in roughly the same ballpark both times. That's the "scalar property," Gibbon's signature finding, and the reason this is called scalar expectancy theory.
What you're actually seeing: when you pick "Watching the Clock," the switch stays open almost the whole time, pulses reliably reach the accumulator, and the response time lands close to your target T. Pick "Absorbed in a Task" instead, same target, same pacemaker, and the switch starts missing pulses. Fewer pulses reach the accumulator per real second, so it takes longer, in real wall-clock time, for the tally to reach the remembered target. Your simulated organism just let more real time slip by without noticing, which is the pacemaker–accumulator model's account of why absorbing tasks make time seem to pass quickly: not because anything sped up, but because attention wasn't being spent tracking the clock. Model: Gibbon, J. (1977). Scalar expectancy theory and Weber's law in animal timing. Psychological Review, 84(3), 279–325. Extended architecture (pacemaker–switch–accumulator–memory–comparator): Gibbon, J., Church, R. M., & Meck, W. H. (1984). Scalar timing in memory.
